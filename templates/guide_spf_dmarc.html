SPF and DMARC setup guide

Updated: November 2026 · For Nigerian small businesses · CyberLord Domain Intelligence

There is a scam that costs Nigerian businesses millions of naira every year, and it takes about ten minutes to block. A criminal registers a lookalike domain or simply sends mail claiming to be from your domain, and your customer, trusting the sender name, pays into their account. SPF, DKIM and DMARC are the three records that stop this. They tell receiving mail servers which systems are allowed to send email for your domain, and what to do with mail that fails the check. This guide explains them in plain English and shows exactly what to add, using examples relevant to businesses in Lagos and across Nigeria.

Why this matters more than it sounds

If your domain has no SPF or DMARC record, anyone on the internet can send email that appears to come from you. Gmail, Outlook and corporate mail systems will still deliver it, because nothing told them otherwise. The criminal does not need to hack your website or your mailbox. They just type your domain into their mail server. For a business that sends invoices, delivery notes or payment requests, this is the perfect fraud channel. Banks and fintechs now treat DMARC as a baseline expectation for legitimate senders, so a missing record also hurts your real mail: your own invoices are more likely to land in spam.

SPF: which servers may send for you

SPF is a single DNS TXT record that lists the mail servers allowed to send on behalf of your domain. Start with a conservative version, publish it, test it, then tighten it. A common starting record looks like this: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net -all. The v=spf1 part identifies the record. Each include: adds a provider you actually use, such as Google Workspace or a bulk mail service. The final -all means any server not listed is not allowed. Keep the record short. Long SPF records that need too many DNS lookups silently fail, which is worse than a short correct one.

DKIM: proving your mail was not altered

DKIM adds a cryptographic signature to your outgoing email. The receiving server checks the signature against a public key stored in your DNS, the same way it would check a signed document. If the signature is valid, the receiving server knows your approved system sent the mail and it was not changed in transit. DKIM is configured inside your mail provider, usually by generating keys and adding one or two TXT records they give you. For Google Workspace, enable DKIM in the admin console and add the selector record. For other providers, look in their dashboard for a DKIM setup page. Without DKIM, SPF alone can be defeated by sending from a subdomain you do not control, so configure both.

DMARC: telling receivers what to do with failures

DMARC is the instruction sheet. It builds on SPF and DKIM and tells receiving servers what to do when a message claims to be from your domain but fails both checks: do nothing, quarantine it, or reject it outright. A DMARC record is a TXT record at _dmarc for your domain, and a safe starting point is v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:you@yourdomain.ng. The p=none policy means monitor only: nothing is blocked yet, but reports arrive at the mailbox you named. Read those reports for a few weeks. They show every sender claiming to be you, including legitimate ones you forgot about and criminals you did not know existed. This reporting is the most valuable part of the whole exercise.

Rollout plan for a small business

Week one: add SPF and DKIM for your main mail provider, and publish DMARC with p=none sending reports to an address you check, such as dmarc@yourdomain.ng. Week two to four: read the reports, fix any legitimate sender that is failing, and note anything suspicious. Week five: move to p=quarantine with a low percentage, for example pct=10, and watch complaints. Then raise the percentage and, once reports are clean, move to p=reject. Rushing straight to reject is how businesses break their own invoice systems, so the slow rollout is deliberate.

Common mistakes in Nigeria

Publishing an SPF record that does not include the service you actually send invoices from, which breaks your own mail. Forgetting that a marketing platform or bulk SMS-to-email service needs its own include. Setting p=reject on day one and discovering your bank alerts or receipt emails stop arriving. Choosing a DMARC reporting address that nobody opens, which defeats the purpose. And assuming your .com.ng or .ng domain is protected because your host "does email", when in fact DNS records are a separate thing from the hosting package. Check with a tool rather than trusting assumptions.
